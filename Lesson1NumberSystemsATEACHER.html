Discussion prompt — suggested answer

“Why can't a computer use denary directly?” — a transistor is most reliable when it only has to distinguish between two clear states (voltage present / absent). Building hardware that reliably distinguished ten different voltage levels would be far more expensive, slower and much more error-prone (electrical noise could easily blur the boundary between, say, “6” and “7”). Two states is the most robust engineering choice.

Common misconceptions to watch for
  • Bit order confusion — students often write place values left-to-right as 1,2,4,8… instead of the correct 128,64,32…1. Anchor this early with the interactive grid.
  • Missing leading zeros — e.g. writing 45 as 101101 instead of the required 8-bit 00101101. CIE mark schemes expect the full bit width specified in the question.
  • Hex letters — some students try to keep using 0–9 past the value 9 (e.g. writing “10” for ten instead of “A”). Drill the A–F mapping directly from the nibble table.
  • Splitting binary into the wrong groups when converting to hex — remind students to group from the right-hand end in 4s.
  • “Hexadecimal is base 6 or base 60” — a name-based mix-up. It's worth explicitly saying “hexa (6) + deci (10) = 16”.

Optional starter & plenary ideas
  • Starter: show a light switch and ask what the two states are (on/off) — bridge straight into transistors as electronic light switches.
  • Mini-whiteboard check: call out a denary number 0–15 and have the student flash the 4-bit binary equivalent, building fluency with the nibble table before the hex section.
  • Plenary: ask the student to find one real hex code around them right now — a colour picker in any design tool, or a device's MAC address in its network settings — to connect the lesson to something tangible.
