Common misconceptions to watch for
  • Forgetting the carry in column addition, especially when a column has three 1s to add (two operand bits plus an incoming carry).
  • Thinking overflow just means “a big number” rather than specifically “the true result exceeds what the register can hold (255 for 8-bit)”. Always have them state the register size.
  • Applying the ×2n/÷2n shortcut blindly for left shifts without checking whether a 1 was shifted off the end — this is exactly what the 195 LSL 1 example is designed to expose.
  • Two's complement: forgetting to invert before adding 1 — a very common slip is adding 1 first. Emphasise the order: invert, then add 1.
  • Reading two's complement as if the MSB were still worth +128 instead of −128 — this is the single most common exam error on this topic.
  • Believing −128 must have a positive “pair” at +128 — worth stating explicitly that 8-bit two's complement is asymmetric (−128 to +127).

Teaching notes & extension ideas
  • The Pac-Man “kill screen” fun fact (Section 2) is genuinely well documented and usually gets a reaction — worth pausing on if the student is into gaming; it's a real, verifiable 8-bit overflow bug.
  • For strong students, ask why two's complement lets a CPU reuse the same adder circuit for subtraction: subtracting B from A is the same as adding A to the two's complement of B. This is beyond the spec but builds real understanding (see Your Turn Q8 in the quiz).
  • If a student finishes early, challenge them to find the largest and smallest values representable in 8-bit two's complement (127 and −128) and explain why the range is not symmetrical.
  • Keep an eye on pacing — if the recap reveals gaps from Lesson 1, it's worth spending 5 extra minutes there rather than rushing two's complement, which depends on binary fluency.
